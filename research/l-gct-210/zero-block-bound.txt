The dimension bound for a fixed 2 by 3 zero block

Claim. Let L be a complex linear space of 5 by 5 matrices with a
common 2 by 3 zero block and perm(J+T)=120 for every T in L. Then
dim L <= 8. The result is a mathematical proof for this support
condition. The desk's unrestricted L-gct-210 remains open.

1. Reduction to a 5-dimensional projection and a 4-dimensional kernel

After permuting rows and columns, write T = [[0,C],[D,E]], where C
is 2 by 2 and D is 3 by 3. We may take dim L = 9 if the bound fails.
The degree-five coefficient of perm(J+zT) is perm(C)perm(D), so one
factor vanishes on L. If perm(C) vanishes, the C projection is an
isotropic subspace of a nondegenerate quadratic form in four
variables and has dimension at most two. The kernel is a level
space on three rows of width five and has dimension at most six by
checked L-gct-156. This contradicts dim L = 9.

Thus the D image P is contained in perm(D)=0. Its kernel K has
directions in the last two columns only. Write those columns x,y.
The first two coefficients of perm(J+zT) give sum(x)+sum(y)=0 and
Q0(x,y) = sum(x)sum(y) - x dot y = 0. On the 9-dimensional
total-sum-zero space Q0 is nondegenerate, so dim K <= 4 and dim P >= 5.

Chan and Ilten, section 7B, show that the only six-dimensional
vector subspaces of the 3 by 3 permanent-zero locus have a fixed
zero row or column. No larger space can exist there, since it
would contain infinitely many six-dimensional subspaces. A fixed
zero row of D gives a fixed 3 by 3 zero block of T, already bounded
by seven. A fixed zero column of D gives a fixed zero column of T,
and checked L-gct-215 bounds the resulting 4 by 5 space by seven.
Thus dim P = 5, dim K = 4, and P has neither a fixed zero row nor
a fixed zero column. The conversion to the rectangular level-space
conditions follows by expanding the permanent in z and completing
each selected matching with the remaining all-ones entries.

2. Row-sum images of the possible D projections

By Chan and Ilten, Proposition 7.4, every allowed five-dimensional
P is, up to row/column permutations, in their product-of-lines
family, their Hirzebruch-surface family, or the latter's transpose.
The other components consist of spaces with a fixed zero row or
column, including the embedded points. We show that the image of
the three row sums is either all of C^3 or a coordinate plane.

For the product-of-lines family take the four annihilating
linear forms on D (row/column indices 0,1,2):

  D11,
  u0 D10 + u1 D12,
  v0 D01 + v1 D21,
  u0 v0 D00 - u1 v0 D02 - u0 v1 D20 + u1 v1 D22,

with (u0:u1), (v0:v1) in P^1. A dependence of the row sums is
equivalent to a row-constant matrix in their span. The middle
row of such a matrix can be nonzero exactly when u0=u1. For
a nonzero outer row the last form must have equal entries at its
two nonmiddle positions, so u0=-u1 is required. The middle-column
coefficients of the two outer rows are proportional to (v0,v1),
whereas their nonmiddle coefficients are proportional to (v0,-v1).
These lines coincide only if v0 v1=0. The two possibilities are
disjoint, and each gives precisely one coordinate row sum zero.

For the Hirzebruch surface use annihilator column spaces

  col 0: span(e1,(u0,0,u1)),
  col 1: span((u0 w,v,-u1 w)),
  col 2: span((u0 w,-v,-u1 w)).

Here (u0,u1) is nonzero and (v,w) is nonzero, with the usual
Hirzebruch-surface scalings from the paper. These spans reproduce
the t2=0 affine chart on p. 675 when u0=w=1, u1=t1, v=t3.
They have constant total rank four for all parameters, hence
give a morphism on the projective parameter surface. Its image
is the closure of the affine chart and thus the whole component.
The minus sign in the third entry of col 1 is required by the
affine chart. The printed homogeneous formula has a plus sign.
The accompanying exact script checks both formulas on the
affine chart and verifies the discrepancy by a counterexample.

For a row-constant matrix in this annihilator, its shared column
vector must be in all three column spaces. When w=0, a dependence
can only come from the middle row, which is then fixed zero.
When w is nonzero, the two one-dimensional columns agree only
if v=0. Then their intersection with col 0 is nonzero only if
u0 u1=0, again yielding a fixed zero row. For the transpose, a
row dependence is a column-constant matrix in the displayed
annihilator. Col 0 contains the all-ones vector iff u0=u1; col 1
or col 2 contains it only if u0=-u1 and v equals u0 w or its
negative, respectively, with u0 w nonzero. These possibilities
are disjoint. Thus outside the excluded fixed-zero cases, any
rank deficiency is exactly a single coordinate sum zero.

3. Common isotropy of the last two columns

Use top indices 0,1 and bottom indices 2,3,4. For D in P, let
A(D) be the first three columns of J+T, whose top two rows are
ones, and let H(D) have zero diagonal and (i,j) entry equal to
the 3 by 3 permanent of A(D) deleting rows i,j. Then the permanent
is X^T H(D) Y for last columns X,Y. Because of the freedom to add
K to any lift of D, x^T H(D) y vanishes on K for every D.

Let s_i be the sum of bottom row i of D. In H's linear part the
(i,j) entry is twice the sum of the s_r with r outside {i,j}.
On K, write U = sum(x) = -sum(y). Since Q0 vanishes, each free
coordinate s_r gives the equation

  2 x_r y_r + U (x_r - y_r) = 0.

At least two bottom row sums are free coordinate values by the
previous section. If U were a nonzero linear form on K, write the
equation as (x_r-U/2)(y_r+U/2)=-U^2/4. Unique factorization of
polynomials on K implies that x_r,y_r are proportional to U
at both such rows. The resulting containing space has dimension
five. On its U=0 slice the other three row coordinates of x,y
have sums zero and pair nondegenerately, giving Q0 rank at least
four on the full containing space. An isotropic four-space is
impossible there. Thus each column sum on K vanishes.

The free rows now satisfy x_r y_r=0 on K. Each must choose one
column identically zero. If two constrained rows choose opposite
columns, the associated six-dimensional pairing space is
nondegenerate. Indeed, the annihilator in 1-perp of the zero-at-r
coordinate subspace is spanned by e_r - 1/5, whose value at the
other row is nonzero. An isotropic subspace has dimension <= 3.
Hence the constrained rows all choose the same zero column, y.

If all three bottom sums are independent, y now has support in
the top two rows and is antisymmetric there. Either y=0 on K
(case A), or the equation x dot y=0 forces x0=x1 on K (case B).
By dimension, K in case A is the entire sum-zero space in x,
and in case B it consists of x sum zero with x0=x1, together
with arbitrary antisymmetric top y. These two forms will also
occur below.

4. Exclusion of the two kernel forms

In case A, adding the sum-zero column x makes all cofactors of
the X column equal a polynomial c(D). Thus the permanent is
c(D) sum(X)=120. Both polynomial factors must be constant,
so c(D)=24 and the directions of X have sum zero. They can be
removed by adding K. All 4 by 4 permanents obtained by deleting
one row from the other four columns are therefore 24.

Their first-degree coefficients force every row sum of the
four-column direction to vanish. Thus the Y direction is zero
in the two top rows and equals -s_i in bottom row i. In the
minor deleting a bottom row, the degree-two coefficient is
minus twice (D_j dot D_k + s_j s_k) for the other two bottom
rows j,k. All three such expressions vanish. Their sum is a
nondegenerate quadratic on the nine D coordinates, with matrix
proportional to (J_3-I_3) tensor (I_3+J_3). Hence dim P <= 4,
a contradiction.

In case B, adding the antisymmetric top y gives a coefficient
proportional to (X0-X1) perm(J_3+D). The polynomial permanent
factor has nonzero constant term, so X0=X1 on the lifts. Use
the antisymmetric y freedom to also arrange Y0=Y1. The other
four columns now have identical top rows. Their two top
cofactors are equal, and varying sum-zero x with x0=x1 makes
the cofactor vector belong to span(1,e0-e1). The equality of
its top entries makes it constant across all rows, so the
same factorization and quadratic argument as in case A apply.

5. The coordinate-plane case

Let p,q be the bottom rows with free sums, and h the bottom row
with sum zero. Set G={0,1,h}. We have yp=yq=0 on K. The two
column sums are zero and the restricted pairing form has a
two-dimensional radical consisting of

  y=0, xp=A, xq=B, x_i=-(A+B)/3 for i in G.

Indeed, y_G ranges over a two-dimensional zero-sum space and
the radical consists of x_G constant. The ambient restricted
space has dimension six and quadratic rank four. Its maximal
isotropic dimension is four, and every maximal isotropic
four-space contains the radical. In particular K contains it.

Let q_i(D) be the sum of 2 by 2 subpermanents of the two bottom
rows other than i. Polarize the quadratic K identity against
the displayed radical. The degree-two D coefficient is

  y_h [(B-2A) q_p + (A-2B) q_q] / 3.

If y_h=0 on K, we have case A or B. Otherwise varying A,B
forces q_p=q_q=0 on P. Since row h has sum zero, this means
row h pairs to zero with each of rows p,q.

If the row-h image has dimension two, it is all the two-dimensional
zero-sum plane. Polarization with the row-h kernel forces the
other two rows of every kernel element to be constant, giving
kernel dimension at most two against dim P=5. If the image has
dimension zero, P has a fixed zero row. The remaining image is
a line C d. Polynomial cancellation forces the other two rows
into d-perp, and dimension five gives P = d-perp x C d x d-perp.
The bilinear form permanent(d,.,.) vanishes on the d-perp plane,
so its 3 by 3 symmetric matrix has rank <= 2. Its determinant
is 2 d0 d1 d2, hence sum(d)=0 forces d proportional to a
permutation of (1,-1,0). Therefore, in a suitable order P is

  D = [[a,a,b],[c,-c,0],[d,d,e]].

6. Exclusion of the last projection

Put u=2a+b, v=2d+e and S=2(ad+ae+bd). In H(D) the offdiagonal
weight within G is A0=6+2(u+v)+S, between G and p is B0=6+2v,
between G and q is C0=6+2u, and between p and q is 6. These
identities follow by expanding the small permanents. The c
terms cancel because the other rows are symmetric in the
first two columns.

Take a linear lift D -> (X,Y), with the all-ones background
added. Write Y_Gsum=3+g, Yp=1+yp and Yq=1+yq with linear g,yp,yq.
Varying X by the radical makes the p and q cofactors equal
the mean of the three G cofactors. In particular

  (B0-2 A0/3) Y_Gsum - B0 Yp + (6-C0) Yq = 0

and the equation with p,q interchanged holds. The cubic
coefficient gives Sg=0, hence g=0. The linear terms give
yp=-u and yq=-v. The quadratic terms then give S=2uv.
This is false, for example at a=d=1, b=e=0. This excludes
the last case and proves the claim.
